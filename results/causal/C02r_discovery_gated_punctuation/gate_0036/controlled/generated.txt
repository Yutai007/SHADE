The latest round of spam complaints will be referred to the industry watchdog, but it is unlikely to introduce tougher sanctions. Some campaigners want to see the ability to send junk mail removed for those who do not abide by the rules. However, the Commission for Racial Equality says the number of cases it will send to the regulator will be small. A spokesman for the commission said that the messages were "deplorable". [More] The Campaign against Electronic Spam gathered thousands of complaints from around the world. The organisation said that the messages were "unacceptable". Mr Phillips, chairman of the Commission for Racial Equality, stated that if the regulators proved to have been largely the work of a small group of spammers, they would take action against them. "There has been a lot of work between the regulator and the CES over the last few months to establish an effective means of dealing with spam," he said. Mr Phillips said that the commission had a duty to take action against people who discriminated against a person's race, religion, colour, nationality (including citizenship) or ethnic or national origins. Spam is defined as unsolicited messages sent to multiple e-mails. Many organisations offer a "blacklist" facility, which can be used to block spam messages. However, some critics argue that spam remains a serious problem and that some spammers have been able to bypass the process. The CES estimates that around 95% of spam does not comply with the UK's Data Protection Act. This means that spam is not treated as a crime under UK law. Some campaigners say that spam is an "unconscionable" form of discrimination. "Spam is nothing more than hate mail in the Information Age," said Mark Smith, the founder of the U.K. Anti-SPAM Alliance. "It degrades the value of e-mail as a communication medium, creates unnecessary social pressure that threatens to drive people away from email and destroys the privacy that individuals have come to expect from the Internet. But it is not a crime."
