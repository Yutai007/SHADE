In many biological systems, groups of cells must collectively migrate to achieve specific goals, such as wound healing or immune response. While the underlying mechanisms guiding such behaviors are not fully understood, the idea of gradient sensing in cells is often considered. However, recent experimental evidence has shown that in some systems, cells can migrate chemotactically without sensing gradients. Here, we present a theoretical model that explains the emergence of such collective chemotaxis. The model shows that, in the absence of gradient sensing, collectives can still migrate towards areas with higher concentrations of a chemical cue due to collective response to local cue fluctuations. We also find that collectives can exhibit a variety of behaviors, including directed migration, diffusion, and random walks, depending on the level of noise in the system. The model suggests that gradient sensing is not always necessary for collective chemotaxis, and that noise can significantly affect the behavior of a collective.
